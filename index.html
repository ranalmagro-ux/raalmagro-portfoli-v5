THE IRONIC BEGINNING

I was bullied for my armpit hair. Somehow, that story led me to Lay Bare.

I got my armpit hair at a young age, and in high school, I was bullied because of it. For a long time, I thought it was a disgusting part of my body — something I had to hide to be acceptable.

Growing up taught me something gentler: that being human means being myself, including the parts of me I once wanted to erase. I didn't know it then, but I carried that idea with me.

Then, ironically, my first job brought me to Lay Bare — a brand built around hair-free mornings. And yep, the irony is not lost on me. But who says hair is automatically a hygiene problem? That association is a social construct. What if confidence can have options? You can accept yourself exactly as you are and still choose to enhance something about yourself because you want to.

That is probably one of the first times I realized how I think as a marketer: I can adapt. I can hold a human truth, understand a brand's point of view, and find the space where the two can meet.

THE ADVOCACY CHAPTER

I eventually realized: I don't just market to market. I market to tell a story.

At some point, I realized I didn't want marketing to be only about making people buy. I wanted to use it to tell a story, give something a purpose, and add something different to people's everyday lives.

Then automotive happened. Funny enough, I barely knew BYD before I encountered the opportunity. I searched, read, learned about the brand and its direction — and I remember thinking, okay, let's do this interview. I like purpose. I like brands that give me something bigger to talk about.

That pulled me toward EV education, mobility and the idea that marketing can help people understand a change that is bigger than a product. I want to see a world that gets greener again. And honestly, if the work I do can help cool the earth a little, even in a small way, that feels pretty cool to me.

Marketing can be political — not because every campaign needs to make a political statement, but because what we choose to normalize, explain, sell and celebrate says something about the world we want to build.

So yes, I market cars. But I also market an idea of a future I want to believe in.

05 / THE BUSINESS SIDE

Creative is good.
Creative that works is better.

I care about the part after “that looks nice.” What did it do? Did it make the brand clearer? Did it earn attention? Did it support a sale, an inquiry, a visit, a conversation or a stronger relationship with the audience?

ROI / THE QUESTION I KEEP ASKING

WHAT DID IT MOVE?

Every piece of marketing should move something — awareness, engagement, traffic, leads, sales, trust or action. I don't want to create just to fill a calendar. I want the work to have a reason to exist.

ATTENTIONDid people stop and notice?
UNDERSTANDINGDid the message become clearer?
ACTIONDid it create an inquiry, visit or next step?
VALUEDid it give the audience something worth keeping?
06 / THE WRITER IN THE MARKETER

Marketing is
what I do.

Storytelling is
how I do it.

Writing is where I feel I belong, but I don't want that part of me to sit separately from marketing. I bring it into the campaign, the caption, the event script, the article, the documentary, the film and the way I explain a product.

The goal is not to make every brand sound like a poem. The goal is to know when a story can make the work more human.
